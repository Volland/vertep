What a certificate proves, and what it does not

Checking rule decisions: profile, support, totality, two engines and a Lean kernel

Formal verification has a way of sounding like a blanket. This article goes through the five checks around a rule decision one at a time and, for each, says what it establishes and what it does not, because the second half is the half that matters to anyone relying on it.

1. The profile: a smaller language

ELPI is a full logic programming language, and most of it is the wrong tool for a policy. The rules profile is the subset the other checks are defined for: no cut, no findall, no input or output, no clocks or randomness, only the host's own builtins, and negation that is stratified (it does not pass through a recursion). A program outside the profile still runs, but its decisions are marked unchecked.

Establishes: the program is written in the part of the language whose meaning does not depend on clause order or the outside world. Does not: that it is correct. And the check is lexical, with a stratification test on predicate names; it does not parse the language, so it is exact about what it flags and conservative about what it cannot see.

2. Support necessity: no padding

A rule lists the statements it rests on. That list could be too short (the host checks every listed statement was read) or too long: a rule can cite a fact it did not need, which makes the explanation look better than it is. Support necessity removes each cited statement in turn, re-runs the rule in a fresh store, and reports any statement whose removal leaves the conclusion standing.

Establishes: every listed statement was needed. Does not: that the list is complete in a causal sense. A rule that reads a threshold from a constant in its own text has no statement for it.

3. Totality: no silent gap

The worst rule is one that quietly does nothing for a case nobody thought of. Totality runs the rule decider on every non-empty subset of up to eight candidate events, under each sample context you give it, in a scratch store, and reports the first offer that nothing answers. An explicit refusal clause, a conclusion of refuse, counts as an answer.

Establishes: for those offers and those contexts, nothing falls through. Does not: anything about other values. It does not reason over all numbers; it runs the samples, and says unknown beyond the eight-candidate bound rather than guessing.

4. Two engines: a second opinion

The engine is lean-elpi, reached through tiramemsu. tiramemsu also has a Datalog engine. For a Horn program, a test runs the same join in both on a random graph and requires the same answers. Establishes: the two agree on that program class, so an engine bug would have to be shared. Does not: cover programs with negation or arithmetic the Datalog side lacks, or any fault in the data they both read.

5. The kernel: a certificate

The strongest check hands the derivation to Lean. For a decision, the exporter writes Lean source: the facts the rule read (the tape), the clause, and a binding of its variables. The Lean module VertepLean.RuleCert states what it means for that instance to derive the conclusion, and a theorem, proved by decide, says it does. The kernel checks the theorem. We checked the checker too: check_sound and check_complete are proved, they depend only on propext and Quot.sound, and the axioms are pinned in the build.

It works, and the test suite shows both sides. A valid certificate is accepted. The same instance with its conclusion changed from approve to refer is rejected by the kernel, and so is one whose amount is edited above the ceiling so that the comparison fails.

Establishes: that, for a clause made of statement lookups and integer comparisons, the recorded conclusion follows from the recorded statements. Does not establish three things, and they should be read before the word “certified” is relied on:

  • It covers a fragment. Clauses of tm.triple atoms and integer comparisons. Negation and other predicates are reported as outside it, never guessed.
  • The clause is the exporter's reading. Lean checks the instance it is given, not that the clause is the program's. The Rust parser that extracts the clause from the program text is trusted.
  • The facts are the tape. That each statement on the tape is really in the store is the host's own check, outside the proof.

There is also a reason it is a fragment. An investigation of lean-elpi's own proof-term layer found that it certifies Horn derivations over a fixed set of facts but has no hook for a host predicate like tm.triple; its answers have to become a tape, and tiramemsu returns the support but not the derivation tree. So the certificate takes the narrow path, and the design records what the wider one would cost.

Reading them together

No check here says a policy is right. They say something smaller and more useful: the rule is in a language whose meaning is stable, it cites what it used and only that, it answers every case we tried, a second engine agrees, and, for the simplest class of rule, a proof kernel agrees the conclusion follows. Each of those removes one way for a decision record to mislead. The docs page on checking rules has the commands, and the first article in this series shows the record the checks are about.
